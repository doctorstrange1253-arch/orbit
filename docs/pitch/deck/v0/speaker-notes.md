# Speaker notes — v0

Five-minute main pitch; backup slides are Q&A only. All quantitative scenarios are estimates, not real bills/traction.

## 01 — Cover (20 sec)

Orbit starts with a simple question: what if the next course began with a skill students actually asked for? We are developing a learner-led platform connecting that demand with mentors, then presenting learning as a visible journey. The product is still a prototype, and today we will distinguish what exists from what remains unproven.

Sources: PRODUCT

## 02 — 01 Problem (25 sec)

The problem hypothesis is specific: adult college learners can struggle to find instruction for a practical topic and to keep going without useful feedback. Our founder and friends have experienced this, but that does not establish that everyone has the problem. We still need a dated example and primary discovery from the first learner group. Our first test is one skill, not every student and every subject.

Sources: FOUNDER

## 03 — 02 Customer insight (25 sec)

Our weakest area is customer evidence. No one-to-one interviews or survey dataset has been supplied, so we will not invent a response count. We have a ready-to-use adult student micro-survey and a mentor conversation script. The sprint targets are fifty to one hundred students and eight to fifteen mentors, but only actual responses will enter this deck. Course requests and written pilot commitments will be stronger signals than favorable opinions.

Sources: FOUNDER

## 04 — 03 Solution (30 sec)

The proposed loop is request, validate, commit and build. Students express a missing topic, others support it, and a mentor decides whether to create a course once the demand is credible. We are not claiming that this is the first such idea or that current catalogs are poor. The distinction we want to test is whether local learner demand can produce a more relevant course and a clearer mentor relationship. Advanced ranked notification waves remain roadmap work.

Sources: PRODUCT

## 05 — 04 How it works (25 sec)

The intended customer path starts with a request and a mentor response. The resulting course becomes finite lessons displayed as a journey, with practice and feedback supporting progress. The image is a real historical prototype capture using test data. It does not prove a live course launch, learning improvement or a seamless current demo. We will show only flows the team can personally repeat before pitch day.

Sources: PRODUCT

## 06 — 05 Progress (30 sec)

We have source-visible request and vote functionality, finite course journey interfaces, and mentor recording and review controls. These are meaningful prototype components. The commercial system is not ready to be presented as an operational marketplace: recurring checkout and actual mentor settlement still need integration. The same honesty applies to generated quizzes and advanced mentor waves. Our next milestone is a small integrated pilot that measures demand, usability, completion and payment readiness rather than assuming traction.

Sources: PRODUCT

## 07 — 06 Market (25 sec)

Our proposed beachhead is adult GEU learners in selected years and branches who need one practical skill. We must confirm that group with the founder and count it using relevant official records, not a national education market figure. Serviceable demand is the relevant population multiplied by reachable share and demonstrated need. Our hundred-paying-student twelve-month figure is a target, not a forecast or current users. Initial mentors need teachable proof, available time and willingness to pilot.

Sources: FOUNDER

## 08 — 07 Business model (35 sec)

Subscriptions target a twenty-percent platform share and an eighty-percent pool for mentors of the learner's enrolled courses. Optional mentor-priced one-to-one sessions have a separate fifteen-percent platform fee. Our working price targets are five hundred ninety-nine and eleven hundred ninety-nine rupees, not live prices. The model deducts gateway charges, video, compute and support from the platform share, not from invented revenue. Tax treatment is informational. Actual bills, usage and refund rules determine contribution, break-even and runway.

Sources: PRODUCT, RZP, CF

## 09 — 08 Action plan (30 sec)

The ninety-day plan starts with customer discovery and five genuine mentor commitments, followed by two reviewed courses and thirty activated learners. We then aim to complete one request-to-launch cycle and test ten paid conversions only if checkout is ready. By day ninety, a second cycle and twenty-five paying learners are targets to evaluate, not promises. Clubs, campus ambassadors, mentor outreach and the TBI network are proposed channels. Every milestone needs a named owner and a measurable result.

Sources: PRODUCT

## 10 — 09 Team (25 sec)

The founder confirms that this is a team, but we still need each person's name, role and actual contribution. We will not invent credentials or attribute all repository work to one person. Engineering must own the integrated product, the content lead must own mentor quality and courses, and the customer lead must own discovery and campus activation. If one person holds more than one role, we will say so. Advisors and photos appear only when genuine and supplied.

Sources: FOUNDER

## 11 — 10 The ask (30 sec)

We propose up to two lakh rupees, the competition's stated ceiling, tied to a measured pilot. Infrastructure and first-course mentor work get priority, followed by campus activation, testing, legal and payment review, equipment and contingency. These are budget allocations, not supplier quotes. With less funding we borrow equipment and reduce paid campaigns before cutting core quality or safety. The first lakh supports a minimum pilot; the next fifty thousand adds a second cycle. Beyond money, we need mentor introductions and pilot access. The panel decides support, and we do not assume an award.

Sources: FORM
